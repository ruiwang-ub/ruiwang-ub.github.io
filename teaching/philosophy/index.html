Teaching Philosophy

I want students to leave each class with something they can use and each course with work they can build on. Sometimes that means a finished website or prototype. Sometimes it means being able to explain a decision about an audience, a message, or a design and support it with evidence.

Bringing my research into the classroom

My research on AI search informs how I teach students to find and evaluate information. As people turn to AI for search, these systems help determine which sources and perspectives they see. I ask students to run a search and record which citations they noticed, which links they opened, and whether the sources supported the answer. I’m also developing an assignment on AI search optimization. Students will compare the pages an AI engine cites, revise a piece of content to make it easier to find and verify, and rerun their searches to see what changes.

In Digital Tools, students bring in a viral short-form video and ask classmates whether they would keep watching, scroll past, or interact with it if it appeared in their feed. We use those responses to discuss how audiences, platform conventions, and algorithmic curation shape attention. Students then make their own short videos and explain how the opening, pacing, and captions suit the audience they want to reach.

Giving students something they can use

In Digital Tools, students build personal websites. In Mobile Media & UX Design, I break the semester project into six stages, from developing an idea and interviewing potential users to prototyping, usability testing, revision, and final handoff. I time each stage to coincide with the concepts and methods we cover in class so students can see how their findings change the design. Loyola students have told me they used course projects to land a full-time job or win an award. I want students to finish the semester with work they can show and the ability to explain the decisions behind it.

Every class can also give students a concept they can use to make a decision. I start my UX course with the platform ecosystem so students can see how platform rules, incentives, and user needs shape the choices designers have. When I teach framing, I split the class into teams tasked with promoting a reusable water bottle. One team emphasizes the benefits of using it, while another highlights the costs of continuing to use disposable bottles. A third group acts as the management team, votes on which pitch it found more persuasive, and explains why. Students come away with a way to use framing theory to design and evaluate messages for a particular audience.

Revising courses as technology changes

I regularly revise syllabi, readings, and assignments across my courses as communication technologies evolve. In Digital Tools, I added dedicated class time for generative AI and short-form video. In Mobile Media & UX Design, I broadened the focus from phones to the platform ecosystems in which people use apps across devices. In graduate Emerging Media Platforms, I added a lab in which students build reusable AI workflows. I’m also developing undergraduate and graduate courses on AI, including a graduate course tentatively titled AI in Emerging Media. As the tools change, I keep returning to questions students can carry from one platform to the next. Who is the audience? What evidence supports a claim? Who can access the message? What are the consequences of a communication decision?

Making room for curiosity

I use learner profiles to learn what students want to explore and give them room to bring those interests into their projects. In my asynchronous graduate course, I schedule midsemester conversations to hear how students are experiencing the course. Students told me that weekly summaries helped them navigate Moodle and that a consistent schedule helped them plan their work. A predictable course structure gives students more room to ask what they want to try next. In Digital Tools, one student told me the course had inspired him to keep exploring the tools on his own. That’s what I hope students carry forward after the semester ends.
